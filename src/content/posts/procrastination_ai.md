I'll be the first to admit I'm probably the least qualified person to write this. I have a DSA playlist sitting in my YouTube Watch Later, a stack of books I keep "setting aside until I have time," and the very article you're reading was itself delayed for longer than I'd like to say. But maybe that's exactly the qualification needed.

Studies consistently find that around 75% of college students consider themselves procrastinators, and about half say it's a chronic problem. If anything, the more surprising statistic is the minority that doesn't.

Procrastination is postponing things that need doing because they feel overwhelming, uncomfortable, or just boring. An exam is two weeks away, you open the syllabus, see three modules from five subjects staring back at you, and suddenly cleaning that corner of your room feels like the most urgent thing in the world. Your brain isn't being lazy. It's looking for something with a lower emotional cost. That avoidance instinct is the actual engine behind most procrastination, not poor time management, not a character flaw.

We eventually get around to it. Sometimes it works out, sometimes it doesn't. But at least the effort was ours.

Here's where it gets complicated.

## When procrastination started looking productive

AI was introduced as a productivity tool. Reduce repetitive work, save time, do more with less. That part is genuinely true. But somewhere in that deal, procrastination found a new shape, and this version is harder to notice because it looks like productivity.

Before AI, if you put off writing a 1500-word essay for two weeks, you'd eventually sit down and write the thing. Messy, maybe. Written at midnight, probably. But your brain did the work of forming arguments, finding the words, structuring a thought. Now that same avoidance ends differently: you open a chat window, spend five minutes prompting an LLM, and walk away with a polished 800-word essay. You feel like you did something. You didn't.

The same pattern plays out with exam prep. You share the syllabus, ask for a "rigorous study plan," admire it for five minutes, and close the tab. Two days before the exam it becomes "can you condense this?" The day before: "just the key points." Two hours before the paper: "the most important points only." Each prompt is a little more desperate than the last, and the AI obliges every time, no questions asked. You never studied. You just watched the plan get smaller.

It shows up in coding too, which hits closer to home for most of us. You don't understand why your program is failing, so you paste the whole thing into a chatbot. Thirty seconds later there's a corrected version in front of you. It runs. You close the tab. You never found out what was actually wrong.

## The work AI shouldn't do for you

Researchers call this cognitive offloading: letting a tool carry part of the mental work for you. That's useful when you're removing busywork. It becomes a problem when the work you're outsourcing is the thing you were supposed to learn in the first place.

Old procrastination delayed the work. New procrastination replaces the work while making it feel done. Your past self who pulled an all-nighter writing that essay was at least building something. The writing, the thinking, the frustration, those were part of the learning. A 2025 RAND survey found that 67% of students said using AI for schoolwork was harming their critical thinking. That's a strange thing to sit with: the majority of students using a tool while believing it's hurting them.

This doesn't mean AI is the villain. AI makes it incredibly easy to remove the wrong kind of friction. Using it to get a better explanation of something you're stuck on, to stress-test an argument you already formed, to debug code you actually wrote and understand, that's fine. Using it to skip forming the argument, or understanding the code, in the first place is the problem.

If you can't explain what the AI just produced in your own words, you haven't learned it. You've just produced an output.

The study plan in your chat history is not the same as knowing the material. The essay in your downloads folder is not the same as having written something. And the DSA playlist in your Watch Later, well, that one's still on both of us.
